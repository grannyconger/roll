# Facilitation script

Minute by minute. Written to be held in one hand during class.

Your moves are the part no software can do. The app handles pacing, data, and
feedback. Everything in this file is yours.

## Standing moves

These run throughout and are not repeated in every segment.

- **Circulate constantly.** You are rarely at the hub. The hub is glanceable from
  across the room on purpose.
- **Wait three seconds** after every question, every time. Silence is doing work.
- **Volunteers first, then pick.** Use the randomizer on the hub for the pick, since
  it draws from students who have not been called on yet.
- **Chime to reset attention.** One chime, then wait. Do not talk over the room.
- **Reference the objective at every transition.** It is posted on both screens all
  period. Point at it.
- **Tradeoffs.** This lesson is built on them. IN versus OUT is a tradeoff, team
  versus individual is a tradeoff, speed versus care on the writing is a tradeoff.
  The line lands naturally here more than usual.

## Before the bell

- Thinking Logs on desks, one per seat.
- Display extended, `/display` on the smartboard, `/teacher` on your laptop.
- Run the assessor health check on the hub. Green before you open the door.
- Wayground vocabulary set loaded and ready in a separate tab.
- Observer link sent to jlowman@greermiddlecollege.org.

---

## 0 to 2, arrival

**Screens:** Display shows join instructions and the class code. Student screens show
sign-in.

**You:** at the door, greeting students as they come in.

**Instructions, given once, from the door or the front:**

> Sign in. Set your name, pick an emoji and a color. Then read the agenda.

Nothing else. The join flow carries itself.

**Watch for:** sign-in failures. If more than two students fail Google auth, call out
the class code and move everyone to the fallback. Do not troubleshoot individuals
while 22 students wait.

---

## 2 to 7, vocabulary warm-up

**Screens:** Display shows the warm-up title and the link. Student screens show a
holding screen.

**You:** run Wayground yourself, as you normally do. The app is out of the way for
these five minutes.

**Bridge out, about 20 seconds:**

> Two of those words decide everything you do for the next hour. Bull means prices
> are going up. Bear means down. Keep them.

**Then:** chime, and tell students to return to the page.

---

## 7 to 11, Learn to Play

**Screens:** Rules appear one at a time on the display, then three practice rounds
run.

The rules reveal progressively while you talk. Do not read them aloud. Say the thing
the screen does not.

**Rule beats, roughly 90 seconds total:**

1. Everyone starts at $100.
2. Each round you choose IN or OUT, before any dice.
3. IN means the market moves your money. OUT earns exactly one dollar, guaranteed.
4. One die sets direction. Four of its six faces mean up.
5. Three dice set the amount.
6. You calculate your own new balance. The site checks it.

**Then three practice rounds, about 45 seconds each.** Nothing is scored and nothing
is written. The point is the loop in their hands.

**During practice round 1,** narrate what is happening as it happens. During rounds 2
and 3, say nothing. Watch the mental math.

**One question before Game 1, with wait time:**

> Four of six faces mean up. So what does that tell you about being IN?

Take a volunteer, then pick one. Expected: IN is more likely to gain than lose. If
nobody gets there, leave it. The mini-lesson returns to it.

**Watch for:** students who enter balances wrong twice in practice. Note them. They
will need you early in Game 1.

---

## 11 to 23, Game 1

Six rounds. No scaffolds, no stems, no help with the writing. This is the baseline
and contaminating it costs you the growth claim.

**Checkpoint rounds are 1, 3, and 5,** plus one round of their choice. On those
rounds students type a reason before the roll.

**Round 1, instruction given once:**

> Pick IN or OUT. Type why. Then we roll.

Say nothing about what a good reason looks like. You have not taught it yet, and the
gap between what they write now and what they write after the mini-lesson is the
entire measurement.

**Every round, your sequence:**

1. Students decide and write.
2. Watch the response counter on the display. When it stalls near full, chime.
3. Lock decisions on the hub.
4. Roll. **Look at the smartboard while it rolls.** Where you look is where they
   look.
5. Students compute. Circulate.

**After round 3, strategy share, about 60 seconds.** Toggle the leaderboard on.

> Top three. What are you doing?

Take two or three answers. Then toggle the leaderboard back off and ask, with wait
time:

> Does any of that predict the next roll?

Expected: no, each roll is independent. If a student argues it does, do not correct
it yet. Name it as something to come back to. It is the recency effect and it is the
centerpiece of the debrief.

**Round 5 doubles.** Announce before they decide:

> This round counts double if you are IN. OUT still earns one dollar.

Then watch the IN count on the hub. That number is loss aversion and risk-taking,
measured live, and it is worth naming later.

**While circulating:** read what students are typing. Do not coach the writing. You
are gathering what the mini-lesson needs to fix.

---

## 23 to 25, feedback returns

**Screens:** Student screens show their feedback on Game 1 responses. Display shows
the prompt and a timer.

**You:** go straight to the students flagged at Level 1 on your hub. Thirty seconds
each, three or four students. This is the highest-value circulation of the period.

**Instruction:**

> Read your feedback. You have two minutes.

**Watch the distribution panel.** If most of the class is at Level 1 and 2, the
mini-lesson leans hard on evidence. If a quarter are already at 3, move faster
through the basics and spend the time on the Level 4 move.

---

## 25 to 39, Claim, Evidence, Reasoning

The instructional core. Fourteen minutes.

**Open with the gap, not the definition:**

> Which was harder, deciding or explaining why?

Volunteer, then pick. Wait.

### The lawyer frame, about 2 minutes

Claim is the verdict. Evidence is the exhibits. Reasoning is the closing argument
that connects them. Exhibits alone do not win a case.

### Model it yourself, about 3 minutes

**This is the modeling beat and it is scored.** Think aloud through one real decision
from Game 1, out loud, including the part where you are unsure. Use a round that
actually happened in this period.

Then do it badly on purpose and ask the class to find what is missing.

### Weak and strong, about 3 minutes

Put a weak example up. Ask what is missing. Then a strong one. Have students point to
the three parts.

Then push a real student response from the Spotlight tray. Use the handle, not the
name. Push one strong IN and one strong OUT, in that order, and say the quiet part:

> Both of these are level threes. One went in, one stayed out. The score is the
> reasoning, not the pick.

### Expected value, about 3 minutes

Up on four of six. Three dice average 10.5. So IN averages about plus $3.50 a round
and OUT is a certain plus $1.

**Question, with wait time:**

> If IN is better on average, why would anyone go OUT?

Expected: an average is not a promise, one bad round can hurt, protecting a lead has
value. This is the tradeoff line, and it is where it belongs.

### The Level 4 move, about 2 minutes

Name the choice you did not make and why you passed on it. Show one example. This is
the single move that separates a 3 from a 4, and most students can make it once they
have heard it named.

### Scoring guide, about 1 minute

Put the four levels up. Students score the weak and strong examples. Expected: 1
and 3.

## 39 to 43, strategy teams

**On the hub:** build teams. Teams appear on the display as a seating chart and on
each student screen.

**Instruction, given once:**

> Find your team. Sit down. You have one minute.

Chime when the minute is up.

**The explanation matters.** Say it plainly, because the reason is the instructional
point:

> You were not grouped by who is winning. You were grouped so that nobody at your
> table played the same way you did.

**Huddle, three minutes.** Each student gets about 45 seconds to defend their Game 1
approach to three people who played differently.

**Circulate.** You are listening for the Level 4 move happening out loud. Note two
students who do it well, because you will call on them in the debrief.

---

## 43 to 61, Game 2

Eight rounds. Scaffolds on: keyword line on the Thinking Log, sentence stem on
screen.

**Checkpoints are 1, 4, and 7,** plus one of their choice.

### Rounds 1 to 4, team decisions

**Instruction:**

> For the next four rounds, your table decides together. Everyone at the table picks
> the same thing. You have extra time to argue about it.

**Do not enforce this.** The system does not check it and neither do you. Compliance
is not the point. What happens inside a student who disagrees and goes along anyway
is the point.

**Circulate and listen.** The arguments at these tables are the best talk of the
period. Note which tables split hard.

Round 4 is the heaviest moment of the period: a team decision, a written checkpoint,
and the probe right behind it. Give the full timer and do not rush it.

### The probe, after round 4

**Instruction:**

> Was there a call in the last four rounds you did not get to make because your table
> ruled you out? Answer honestly. Nobody at your table sees this.

Students who say yes name the round, what they would have done, and why. The system
shows them what their own call would have been worth against the roll that actually
happened.

**Give this a beat.** Twenty seconds of silence after the counterfactual lands. Then:

> Hands up if your own call would have beaten your table's.

Do not editorialize. Let the hands do it. Come back to it in the debrief as herding.

### Rounds 5 to 8, individual

Normal pace. Round 7 doubles.

**Compare to Game 1 aloud** after the round 7 announcement:

> Last game, on the double round, we had [N] in. What about now?

---

## 61 to 65, peer scoring

**Screens:** two anonymous responses, plus the scoring guide.

**Instruction:**

> Score two classmates against the same guide. Name what is missing or what works.
> One sentence.

**Circulate.** When a student is unsure, ask them which of the three parts they can
find. That question does the teaching.

---

## 65 to 68, bias survey

**Push it from the hub.**

**Instruction:**

> Seven traps. Check any you felt today. Then one sentence about the strongest one.

Give them two minutes, then take two answers aloud. Volunteer first, then pick.

---

## 68 to 74, mind traps debrief

**Screens:** the seven traps.

Do not lecture the list. Students have the definitions in front of them and just
reported their own. Work from their data.

**Three beats, about two minutes each:**

1. **Recency.** Return to the streak argument from the Game 1 strategy share. Name
   it now. "Each roll is independent" lands differently once they have lost money to
   the belief.
2. **Loss aversion and risk-taking.** The two double rounds. Who went IN when behind?
   Ask one of them why, with wait time.
3. **Herding.** The probe. Call on one of the students you noted during the huddle.

> You wanted out. Your table said in. What happened in your head when you went along?

This is the moment of the lesson. Protect the time for it. If you are running long,
cut beat 1, not beat 3.

---

## 74 to 81, exit ticket

**Screens:** the scenario, no stem, no keyword field. Vocabulary still available.

**Instruction:**

> On your own. No stem, no notes from your table. Claim, evidence, reasoning.

Silent and individual. Circulate but do not coach. This is the transfer measure.

**Watch the counter.** If submissions stall at 20 of 24 with two minutes left, chime
and give a one-minute warning.

---

## 81 to 85, closure

**Point at the objective.** It has been on the screen for 85 minutes.

**Two questions, collected:**

> Can you justify a conclusion with a claim, evidence, and reasoning?
>
> Name one thing you did differently in the second game than the first.

Take two answers aloud. Collect the rest.

**Close on the tradeoff.** Every round today was a tradeoff, and so is every real
investing decision. The guaranteed dollar was never wrong. It was just a different
tradeoff from the one most of the room took.

---

## 85 to 90, reserve

If you arrive here on time, go back to the herding beat. It rewards extra minutes
more than anything else in the period.

---

## What to do if things go wrong

**Assessor is down.** Say nothing to students. The screens switch to self-assessment
on their own. Self-scoring against the guide is a real skill and the lesson holds.

**You are 10 minutes behind at the mini-lesson.** Cut Game 2 to six rounds from the
hub. Do not cut the mini-lesson and do not cut the exit ticket.

**A student is stuck on mental math repeatedly.** The app reveals after two
attempts. Do not stand over them. Note it and move on.

**The room gets loud during team deliberation.** That is the design working. Chime
only if it crosses into off-task.

**A student is off-task browsing.** You are circulating constantly, which is the
control. Address the individual, never the whole class.

**A student misses the lock entirely.** They are OUT automatically and gain the
dollar. Say nothing unless they ask. The system already handled it.
