## Standard

**SC Personal Finance 5141, Standard A, Managing Personal Finances:** The student
will analyze how human behaviors impact financial decisions.

Supporting indicators from the same course:

- Define basic investment concepts.
- Explain the relationship between risk and the rate of return and the importance of
  risk management.

The standard is displayed on the classroom display and on every student screen for
the full period, and is referenced aloud at each transition.

## Learning objective

**I can justify a conclusion using a claim, evidence, and reasoning.**

### Sub-objectives, sequenced basic to complex

1. I can define bull market, bear market, risk, and expected value.
2. I can decide whether to be IN or OUT and calculate the result.
3. I can state a claim about my decision.
4. I can support my claim with evidence from the game.
5. I can explain why my evidence supports my claim.
6. I can name the choice I did not make and explain why I passed on it.
7. I can apply this reasoning to a situation outside the game.

Objectives 1 and 2 are reached in the warm-up and Learn to Play. Objectives 3 through
5 are taught explicitly in the mini-lesson between games. Objective 6 is the Level 4
target. Objective 7 is the exit ticket.

## Prior knowledge

Students have completed the investment vehicles unit and can name common investment
types. They have not been taught claim-evidence-reasoning in this course. The lesson
assumes no prior CER instruction, which is why Game 1 runs with no scaffolds and the
mini-lesson comes after students have felt the need for it.

## Expectations for student performance

Every student writes a justification at four points in each game. The target is Level
3 or higher on at least 3 of 4 responses in Game 2. The target is stated to students
at the start and shown on the scoring guide, which stays available all period.

Students are told plainly that the dice decide the winner and the reasoning decides
the score. A student who loses every round can earn full marks.

## Assessment plan

Student performance is measured in **five** ways, all aligned to the standard and all
carrying explicit criteria.

| Mode | What it measures | Criteria |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Written justifications, 8 per student | Ability to justify a conclusion | 4-level CER guide, visible to students all period |
| Mental math entries, 14 per student | Applying the risk and return rule | Correct or incorrect against a computed value |
| Game decisions, 14 per student | Risk tolerance and consistency | Behavioral pattern, not right or wrong |
| Peer review, 2 per student | Applying criteria to someone else's work | Same 4-level guide |
| Exit ticket, 1 per student | Transfer to a new situation | Same 4-level guide, no scaffolds |

### Measurement criteria

The 4-level guide is the single criterion set for all written work:

| Level | Descriptor |
| --- | --- |
| 4 | Claim, evidence, reasoning, plus the alternative considered and rejected |
| 3 | Claim, specific evidence from the game, reasoning that connects them |
| 2 | A reason, but no fact from the game |
| 1 | A choice or a feeling only |

### How results inform instruction

Scores arrive live during the period, so instruction adjusts within the lesson rather
than after it. A high count at Level 1 after Game 1 means the mini-lesson leans
harder on evidence and runs the practice beat twice. A high count at Level 3 means
the mini-lesson moves quickly to the Level 4 move.

After the period, the Game 1 to Game 2 comparison establishes whether explicit CER
instruction changed justification quality, which answers the professional goal
attached to this lesson. The exit ticket establishes whether it transferred.

### Goal statement

By November 20, after explicit instruction in justifying a conclusion using Claim,
Evidence, and Reasoning, 70 percent of students in both Personal Finance sections
will score Level 3 or higher on at least 3 of 4 required CER entries in the
scaffolded game, measured against the 4-level guide and compared with their own
unscaffolded entries from the first game.

## Differentiation and accommodation

Planned for in advance, not improvised.

**Pacing.** Every timed task shows time remaining. Students who finish a response
early receive an extension prompt on their own screen rather than waiting, so faster
learners go deeper instead of idling.

**Scaffolding, faded deliberately.** Game 1 has none. Game 2 provides a keyword line
on paper and a sentence stem on screen. The exit ticket removes both. The fade is the
instructional design, not a convenience.

**Reading and attention.** One task is visible at a time. There is no ambient motion
during reading or writing. A dyslexia-friendly font can be toggled by any student at
any time without asking. The vocabulary list is reachable from every screen,
including during the exit ticket, so a student who forgets a term is never stuck.

**Mathematics.** Mental math is scaffolded on a wrong answer: the setup is shown
before the answer is, and the answer appears after two attempts so no student stalls
publicly or privately.

**Entry points.** A student arriving late joins at the class average balance and
plays from the current round, so arriving late is not a competitive penalty.

**Grouping.** Strategy teams are built from Game 1 data so that each team holds four
different approaches and reasoning strength is spread evenly. No team is composed
entirely of students who struggled with the writing.

**Feedback.** Every student receives specific written feedback on every checkpoint
within seconds, which is not otherwise achievable with 24 students and a single
teacher.

## Closure

The final four minutes return to the objective as it was posted at minute zero.
Students answer whether they can now justify a conclusion with a claim, evidence, and
reasoning, and name one thing that changed between the two games.

The reflection is collected, so closure produces evidence rather than only a feeling.

---

## Connection to prior and future learning

**Prior.** The investment vehicles unit gave students the asset types. This lesson
gives them the behavior that surrounds those choices.

**Future.** The CER structure taught here is reused in the credit unit and in the
course's final project. Justifying a conclusion is a course-wide thinking skill, not
a one-lesson activity, and students are told so.

**Across disciplines.** Claim, evidence, and reasoning is the same structure used in
their English and science courses. The connection is named aloud, because students
rarely make it on their own.
